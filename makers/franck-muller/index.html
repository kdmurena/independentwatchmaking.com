Franck Muller was one of the pioneering master watchmakers of the independent movement, and among the first of the modern era to build a house under his own name. Born in Switzerland on 11 July 1958 to an Italian mother and a Swiss father, he showed an early fascination with mechanical objects and enrolled in watchmaking school as a teenager, completing four years at the Geneva School of Watchmaking in the early 1980s. His brand is no longer a one-man atelier, and its present-day scale is far beyond what this catalogue normally covers. It is catalogued here for the man and for what he started, in the same spirit as Daniel Roth, Roger Dubuis and Gérald Genta.

After school Muller made his name in restoration. Wikipedia places him close to the independent watchmaker Svend Andersen in Geneva, handling watches from the Patek Philippe collection, with clients that were mostly private collectors and museums. The brand's own account says auction houses and collectors worldwide sent him complicated pieces to restore. Working inside historic complications taught him how they were built, and he began to make his own. By the brand's account he presented his first wristwatches in 1983, all with complication movements he had made himself, and it credits him with the first tourbillon wristwatch whose tourbillon could be seen from the dial side rather than only through the back. Wikipedia dates his first tourbillon wristwatch to 1984, so the exact year is given differently by different sources. In a period when few houses could make such complications, a young watchmaker building them under his own name was unusual and became influential.

In 1991 Muller founded the House of Franck Muller in Geneva with Vartan Sirmakes, born in Constantinople in 1956 into a family of Armenian jewellers, a jewel setter and case maker who ran his own Geneva workshop and who chose Muller as his partner for high-end watches. The partnership paired Muller's complications with Sirmakes's case-making and jewellery skills and his commercial drive. The first Franck Muller watches, with the curved Cintrée Curvex case inspired by 1930s tonneau watches, became popular in Europe and the United States. In 1993 the house unveiled its first 'World Premiere': a watch combining a split-seconds chronograph, minute repeater, perpetual calendar and temperature indicator. A new World Premiere followed each year, and Muller became known as the 'Master of Complications'. The company launched its own watch fair in 1998, and by 2003 it employed almost 500 people at Genthod near Geneva and was reported to make close to 49,000 watches a year.

Muller did not stay. In October 2003 the company announced that he had left 'for personal reasons', remaining a shareholder and board member while Sirmakes directed the management. A month later, at a Geneva press conference on 20 November 2003, Muller said he was neither ill nor on holiday, that he felt betrayed by his associate, and that he was demanding the dissolution of Watchland. Press reports said he had sued Sirmakes in June 2003 over the management of the company's finances, and Sirmakes had counter-sued for libel. In spring 2004 the rival suits were still before the Swiss courts, the company wanted Muller's shares sold so that a planned stock market flotation could go ahead, and the company's finance chief said its roughly 550 staff had remained with Watchland rather than following Muller. The company has stated that it owns the Franck Muller Geneve brand name.

The feud ended in October 2004. Muller withdrew his accusations against Sirmakes and the group's management, both sides withdrew all legal proceedings, and the group kept him on as a consultant. What happened to his shareholding afterwards, and what he has done as a watchmaker since, we could not verify from the sources available to us, so this listing does not say. The brand that carries his name is today a privately held Swiss group, Franck Muller Watchland, based in Genthod, and it is not independent in the sense used by this catalogue. It still markets the 'Master of Complications' line, with tourbillons, perpetual calendars, minute repeaters, skeletonised and Crazy Hours jumping-hour watches, and the Revolution 3 tri-axial tourbillon of 2004, and it sells through a network of retailers. Prices are not published and are given on enquiry.

We include Franck Muller as an exception because of the man's place in history: a watchmaker in his early thirties who founded a house under his own name in 1991, at a time when almost no one did, and who helped pave the way for the generation of independents that followed. The listing honours the founder's career and the watches he made before and as he built the house. It does not endorse the present-day brand, and the Methodology page records this exception.
