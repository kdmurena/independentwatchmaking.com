Schnelle is the watch brand of Thomas Schnelle, a one-person atelier near the Theresienwiese in Munich, Germany. Schnelle was born in 1970 in Bad Oeynhausen, trained first as a toolmaker and then as a watchmaker, finishing in 1995 as Germany's best watchmaking trainee in the national practical competition and joining a talent-support programme. He has been self-employed since 1991, when, aged 21, he began supplying Munich's Chronoswiss with a movement model with a pin-pallet escapement, the prototype for a numbered series of 99. He freelanced for the brand on repairs, dial and case design, prototypes, 3D renderings and print work, according to Worn & Wound until Chronoswiss was sold in 2012, and for nearly 20 years he also repaired Cartier Tank watches for the Richemont group.

His own watch has its origins in prototypes begun in 2019, intended for a local craft exhibition in Munich. The pandemic ended the event early, but the watch was then featured by WatchesTV, which brought collectors and wide attention, and Worn & Wound reports that he sold his first 20 watches in 2021. The Art Deco watch is a small-series, hand-wound wristwatch in a rectangular case that, according to Worn & Wound, measures about 23 mm wide and 7.5 mm thick with a 48 mm lug-to-lug, machined in his workshop in sandblasted stainless steel. It has a U-shaped sapphire crystal with angular edges on top, a screwed sapphire caseback that shows the movement, and a 17 mm strap that tapers to 14 mm. Monochrome describes it as water-repellent, not water-resistant.

The movement is a vintage Longines 9LT, sourced through auctions and industry contacts and dated by Monochrome to roughly 1945 to 1955, with 17 jewels, a screw balance with a Breguet overcoil hairspring and 18,000 vibrations per hour, and a power reserve of about 43 hours. Each movement is cleaned, lubricated, fitted with replacement parts where needed, and its screw heads and surfaces are polished back to their original shine. An optional salmon gold-tone finish is available. The dial is offered in German silver, ruthenium grey, black or salmon, with an indirect small seconds, and bespoke numerals, fonts and colours are common requests. The brand says everything except the sapphire glass and the movement is made in-house.

Schnelle makes about one or two watches a month and has said that orders have built up into a backlog. He continues freelance work for clients, including a large brand, to stay independent. Worn & Wound reported a price of about USD 2,000 plus tax, with a bespoke dial adding a few hundred dollars. The brand's own page lists no price and directs buyers to contact the maker, and Monochrome says buyers are sent to Instagram for current options. Worn & Wound notes that about 90 percent of buyers are men even though the watch was designed with women in mind.

The workshop's portfolio, shown on the maker's own site, covers more than watches. It includes the Chronoscope Minutecounter chronograph, a 42 mm design shown at Baselworld 2009, the Sirius dial designs for Chronoswiss from 2011, instruments and two wristwatches for the Dutch car maker Spyker, the Tachoscope made for Audi's 100th anniversary, a concept jumping-seconds watch named Sauterelle that never reached series because of movement problems, and a range of small-series Micromove watch winders. The atelier also repairs and services wristwatches and large clocks of almost any make, refurbishes cases, and does CNC milling and prototype work, including air ducts for Infineon. The studio has no fixed opening hours, so visits are arranged in advance.
