Nord Zeitmaschine

Switzerland
Foundedc.1997 (brand discontinued; founder d. 2020)
LocationNorthern Switzerland
PriceCHF 15,000–24,000 (secondary market)
Volume~30/yr at peak; discontinued, all pieces sold
Nord Zeitmaschine on Instagram ↗ Brand discontinued — no official site currently active.

Daniel Nebel, born in 1971 in northern Switzerland, was not a classically trained watchmaker but an industrial mechanic, working on one-of-a-kind bespoke machinery for the aviation industry, truck tyre rim production, and pipeline manufacturing — large-scale engineering, not micromechanics. Along the way he built genuine expertise in control engineering, hydraulics, and electronics, and a particular talent for building prototype machines. Around 1995, alongside his day job at a factory — which he kept for a steady income his entire life — he began experimenting with building his own wristwatches, setting up a small home workshop by 1997 and completing his first watch there.

Realising he would need a CNC machine to build anything more sophisticated, and finding the ones then available prohibitively expensive, Nebel simply built his own: an eighteen-month project that produced a four-axis machine at a time when most amateur setups ran on three. He then built the twelve tools he needed from it, and four further CNC machines of his own design, including one that could print dials automatically. He named the brand Nord Zeitmaschine — 'north time machine' in German — for the northern Swiss region where he was born and worked, noting with some amusement that his own initials, reversed, bookended the word 'Nord.' Working almost entirely alone, he machined his own cases, dials, crowns, module plates, gear wheels, bridges, and buckles by hand, outsourcing only the base ETA 2824 automatic movements, crystals, and the galvanising of his blued hands — enough handwork that he could complete only around 30 watches a year.

His breakthrough design, the Variocurve, solved a problem Nebel posed to himself: every conventional watch display, however exotic, still rotated around a fixed centre. After testing and rejecting various lever systems, he arrived at a pair of counter-rotating eccentric cams paired with two segment displays running counter to the hands, so that the minute hand traces a different curve and moves at a different speed throughout each hour, with sliding hour and date displays at eight and five o'clock. Cased in forged steel at 43.6mm by 15.8mm, running on the automatic Calibre NORD N2 (an ETA 2824 base) with 25 jewels and 42 hours of reserve, the Variocurve was built across four versions and priced around 15,000 Swiss francs.

The Quickindicator followed, built around simpler gearing but a harder engineering problem: giving the minute hand, mounted on a counterweighted arm to stay balanced under gravity in any position, a track shaped like three circles in one, so that it visibly races around three times the normal distance and speed of an ordinary hand — earning it, in Nebel's own words, the fastest minute hand in the world. Sliding hours sat at three o'clock and a sliding date at nine, in a 44.3mm by 15.6mm forged steel case built across four versions on the same 25-jewel automatic base.

Two further families pushed the idea further still. The Freesdial did away with a fixed dial altogether: a long minute hand pivots around a mobile wheel while the minute numerals themselves sit on two moving segments, with the hour on a rotating counter at four o'clock and, in some versions, a second time zone and day/night indication on a further counter at eight, priced around 24,000 Swiss francs. The rarest and most ambitious, the Crossnroll, replaced the hand altogether with a red arrow on a disc that continuously orbits the dial like a slow pendulum, always pointing to the correct time, with the minutes read from a separate wheel beneath it — built in only two known versions and never formally offered for sale.

Nebel died on September 20, 2020, at 49, following several months of undisclosed illness — news that only reached most of the watch world the following January, via a reader's comment on an in-memoriam post. Fréret-Roy, the Paris retailer that had exhibited his work annually since 2016, sold its last four Nord Zeitmaschine watches within months of his death; none have been available new since. Working entirely alone, without an heir to the workshop or a Baselworld booth beyond his own, Nebel left behind a small, genuinely singular body of work that today survives only in the hands of the collectors who found it in time.
