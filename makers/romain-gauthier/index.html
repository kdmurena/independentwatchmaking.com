Romain Gauthier

Romain Gauthier was born in 1975 in Le Sentier, in the Vallée de Joux — the cradle of fine Swiss watchmaking — to parents who both worked in the watch industry: his father as a precision mechanic at Lemania, Audemars Piguet, and Dubois Dépraz, his mother as a secretary at Lemania and Audemars Piguet. Despite that upbringing, he wasn't initially drawn to watches themselves; his early passion was for technical and mechanical things generally, discovered as a student when he found his forte creating machine parts on a lathe. He trained in precision mechanics, earning a diploma as a constructor of precision machinery, and in 1997 began his first job as a machine programmer-operator at François Golay, where over several years he helped build one of the best-equipped and most efficient CNC production floors in Europe. It was while working there that he had what he describes as an epiphany: reflecting that he had been born in the cradle of fine Swiss watchmaking and possessed the exact skills to build a watch of his own, he began sketching designs in his free time starting in 1999, the ideas growing steadily more serious over the following months.

A pivotal 2002 visit to the atelier of Philippe Dufour proved decisive. Gauthier's central question to Dufour was whether one person could design, build, and market their own watch under their own identity, from their own workshop — and through their conversations, Dufour convinced him it was possible. Dufour later pushed him, as his first watch neared completion, to go beyond merely 'very good' or 'excellent' — to ask, for instance, why settle for hand-polished flat bevels when hand-polished rounded bevels were possible. Determined now to build not just a watch but a company, Gauthier pursued an MBA at Lausanne Business School in his spare time, submitting the business plan for his own watch brand as his final thesis, passed with distinction in 2002. His former employer, learning of the ambition, granted him after-hours access to the factory's own tools and machines, allowing Gauthier to develop his first movement's components at night, on weekends, and during holidays over the next several years — including custom-designing his own hairspring when he couldn't find an existing one suited to his design.

He founded Montres Romain Gauthier in Le Sentier in 2005, after three years of work conducted largely in secret, and within the first year hired his first watchmaker to hand-finish and assemble the movement he had been developing, Calibre HM 2206. The brand's motto, 'The Evolution of Tradition,' captures his stated approach ever since: deep respect for historical watchmaking technique combined with genuinely original engineering, where every complication he pursues — a caseback winding crown, a constant-force mechanism, a bidirectional micro-rotor — must make personal sense to him and bear all the hallmarks of a Romain Gauthier creation, in engineering and in finishing, rather than being adopted simply because it exists.

The Prestige HM, launched at Baselworld 2007, was the brand's debut: an in-house movement visible through the display back, wound via an innovative pusher set into the case band rather than a conventional crown (so the watch could be wound while still worn on the wrist), off-centred hour and minute indications, and finger-bridges characteristic of Vallée de Joux movement architecture. The Prestige HMS followed in 2010, adding a seconds hand and a partially openworked dial, and the Prestige HMS Natural Titanium the following year laid the groundwork for the more technical, high-tech-material Freedom collection. The Logical One, unveiled in 2013, became the watch that put Romain Gauthier definitively on the horological map and won Best Men's Complication at that year's Grand Prix d'Horlogerie de Genève: a 21st-century reinvention of the historical chain-and-fusée constant-force mechanism, with a snail cam replacing the traditional cone-shaped fusée, a ruby-link chain running between synthetic sapphire mainspring-barrel inserts to eliminate friction, a dial-side visible balance, and the same pusher-winding system introduced on the Prestige HM — delivering constant force across the full 46-hour power reserve from a 359-component movement with 63 jewels (26 dedicated to the constant-force chain alone), beating at 28,800 vibrations per hour. The titanium constant-force bridge alone takes roughly 20 hours of hand-finishing to complete.

A new manufacture building in Le Sentier followed in 2014, the same year as the Logical One Secret Diamonds and the launch of the Exception collection — unique pieces showcasing virtuoso decorative artisanship from some of the finest specialist craftspeople in the trade. Between 2015 and 2016, as the Freedom collection expanded with further models, the Fondation de la Haute Horlogerie formally recognised the maison as a genuine 'high watchmaking' house. The Insight Micro-Rotor arrived at Baselworld 2017 as Gauthier's first automatic watch, three years in development: a bidirectional 22-karat solid gold micro-rotor, visible from both dial side and caseback, that converts wrist motion to mainspring energy regardless of which direction it turns — requiring Gauthier to engineer a custom reversing gear whose centre axis glides side to side along a curved cut in two steel plates as the micro-rotor changes direction — delivering an 80-hour power reserve with off-centre hours, minutes, and small seconds, also offered in a Squelette version with 156 hand-bevelled internal angles cut into titanium. The C by Romain Gauthier subsequently paired the ergonomics of a casual titanium case with the same highly visible manufacture movement architecture, a more everyday-wearable take on the brand's technical language.

Today, Manufacture Romain Gauthier occupies roughly 700 square metres (8,000 square feet) of purpose-built premises in Le Sentier, housing technical, logistics, and production departments alongside dedicated watchmaking and decoration ateliers, blending time-honoured hand tools with a state-of-the-art CNC machine park that allows the company to design, produce, decorate, assemble, regulate, and test every movement entirely in-house — the great majority of components made from raw material to finished part on site. Annual output remains deliberately minute, at roughly 60 watches a year, priced between CHF 30,000 and CHF 150,000, sold to collectors specifically drawn to the extraordinary density of hand-finishing and the sincerity of Gauthier's insistence that a complication only belongs in a Romain Gauthier watch if it means something to him personally. Chanel holds a 20% minority interest in Romain Gauthier.
