Jean Dunand is a Swiss brand of unique pieces: every watch is meant to be a one-off, and every collection a patented world first. It was founded in 2003 by master watchmaker Christophe Claret and Thierry Oulevay, the watch entrepreneur whose Art Deco vision shaped its designs and who died in 2013, and it is best known for the Tourbillon Orbital, the Shabaka perpetual calendar minute repeater and the Palace, a tourbillon chronograph with a linear GMT that many judged the best watch of Baselworld 2010. The name honours the Art Deco artist and craftsman Jean Dunand, and the brand's designs draw openly on Art Deco and Art Nouveau.

Oulevay was a watch entrepreneur who had worked at Piaget and re-created the historic brand Bovet, which he left in 2001. Monochrome says he met Claret that year and that together they planned a brand aimed at the highest level of watchmaking, in which every model would be a world first and every piece unique; Oulevay supplied the Art Deco aesthetic and pushed Claret to build an orbital tourbillon, then thought close to impossible. Europa Star's 2008 profile describes the charter: each piece unique, each new collection a world first, a new collection roughly every two years, production in the tens of pieces, and distribution through a few exclusive resellers. Oulevay died unexpectedly in 2013, and the brand went quiet for a time.

The first watch was the Tourbillon Orbital. Sources differ on its debut, giving 2004 and 2005, and Europa Star says it followed nearly five years of development. Its patented IO 200 movement, designed and built by Claret and named after Jupiter's moon Io, is a manually wound calibre of 33 mm by 10.4 mm with 215 parts, a 21,600 vibrations per hour balance and a claimed power reserve of about 110 hours. A one-minute flying tourbillon orbits the dial once an hour, hours, minutes and moon phases are shown, and the watch is wound and set with a folding key through the case back instead of a crown. The 45 mm case came in rose gold, white gold or platinum, and every watch was made to order with its own dial, a 3 cm disc in gold, rare hardstones, fossils or Art Deco designs, among them ruby heart, Burma jade, pietersite, fossilised coral and black opal.

The Shabaka, presented in 2007 and named after an Egyptian pharaoh of the 25th dynasty, pairs an instantaneous perpetual calendar, shown on four rotating cylinders, with a minute repeater chiming on two cathedral gongs, a leap-year indicator and a moon phase. Claret patented the instant-change calendar. Its original angular Art Deco case worked poorly with the stone and artisanal dials used on the Orbital, so a version shown at Baselworld 2015 moved to a round 47 mm case in white gold, pink gold or palladium, powered by the manually wound CLA88QP with 721 components, an 18,000 vibrations per hour beat and a 45-hour reserve. Each dial is a one-off created with its owner, at around CHF 465,000 depending on customisation. Europa Star also records a Grande Complication series limited to six pieces, with 827 components and six months of work for each watch.

The Palace was presented at Baselworld 2010 at a price of about US$410,000 and was widely named the standout watch of the fair. Its case, 48.2 by 49.9 mm and 16.65 mm thick in titanium with white or red gold options, takes its cues from the Crystal Palace and the Eiffel Tower, with side windows, hollow arched lugs and tiny lattice structures. The manually wound CLA 02CMP calibre, with 703 components, combines a flying tourbillon turning once a minute, a 60-minute monopusher chronograph, a retrograde linear slide GMT whose arrow flips after twelve hours, and a 72-hour linear power reserve. Quill & Pad, writing in 2015, called it a handful of unique pieces rather than a proper limited edition. A Palace dated around 2020 with a March 2020 guarantee and movement number 010 sold at Phillips in July 2022 for HK$693,000, well above an estimate of HK$120,000 to 240,000, which suggests the brand was still delivering watches at that point.

This listing has no official website because the brand's own web address could not be confirmed, and the sources used do not say who owns or runs Jean Dunand today or where it is based; the brand is shown here as Swiss on the strength of the sources' Swiss production. The Master Horologer profile could not be retrieved, and the GPHG page gave only a title.
