Credor's Micro Artist Studio and its sister atelier, Shizukuishi Watch Studio, are owned by Seiko — but together they are, in every practical sense, among the most independent-in-spirit workshops in all of watchmaking, and among the very few places on earth where a lineage runs directly back to Philippe Dufour himself. The Credor name was registered in 1974 (from the French crête d'or, 'pinnacle of gold'), launched as Seiko's precious-metal luxury line; within a decade it was producing jewelry watches priced as high as ¥220 million. But by the late 1990s Seiko had lost the know-how to build serious hand-finished mechanical watches, and in 2000 it built Micro Artist Studio from nothing to recover it — tucked into a corner of Seiko Epson's factory in the mountain town of Shiojiri, Nagano, one of two elite ateliers making up what Seiko now calls the Shinshu Watch Studio (the other, Shizukuishi Watch Studio in Iwate, was established in 2004 to handle Credor's mechanical models). Its founding watchmakers taught themselves largely by studying Philippe Dufour's Simplicity in a Ginza shop window, then, in 2002, two of them traveled to Dufour's own atelier in the Vallée de Joux — where, in Dufour's own recollection, 'watchmakers know no borders,' and he personally showed them his techniques for beveling, finishing, and decoration. That direct mentorship, extended further by finisher Ronald de Carle, transformed the studio's work within a few years into something the horological press now routinely discusses in the same breath as Dufour's own watches.

Micro Artist Studio has never numbered more than about ten craftsmen at a time, several holders of the WorldSkills Competition gold medal or Japan's Medal with Yellow Ribbon for master craftsmanship, working alongside a neighboring jewelry studio that handles gem-setting and precious metals — a scale and intimacy far closer to a Genevan atelier than to an industrial manufacture. Its specific charge within Credor is the brand's Spring Drive art pieces within the Masterpiece Collection, and the model lines that have come out of its benches read as a history of Japanese haute horlogerie in miniature: the Node Spring Drive Sonnerie (2006), Japan's first complication watch, built around a silent glide-wheel governor exploiting Spring Drive's inherent quiet; the Node Eichi (2008), introducing the world-first Torque Return System on the new Caliber 7R08; the Spring Drive Minute Repeater (2011), released for Seiko's 130th anniversary; and the Eichi II (2014), built on a concept its makers call the perfection of simplicity — its spare, hand-finished porcelain dial frequently cited by Western critics as among the most beautiful in modern watchmaking, evoking Dufour's own work in spirit if not in name. Engraver Kiyoshi Terui, a Medal with Yellow Ribbon recipient, has become one of the studio's defining figures, spending upward of 100 hours hand-carving a single dial.

The piece that made Credor's artisanry famous outside Japan, the Fugaku Tourbillon, was unveiled at Baselworld 2016: Seiko's first tourbillon in 135 years, limited to just eight pieces, its Caliber 6830 the world's smallest tourbillon movement by volume at launch. As a mechanical rather than Spring Drive watch, its movement was assembled at sister-studio Shizukuishi — but Terui's hand himself engraved its three-dimensional gold rendering of Hokusai's Great Wave off Kanagawa, ridges varying from 0.5mm to 1.6mm for real depth, while the reverse tourbillon bridge is shaped in outline as Mount Fuji, fugaku being an old, poetic name for the mountain; designer Nobuhiro Kosugi, the first watch designer ever named a Contemporary Master Craftsman by the Japanese government, integrated tourbillon, engraving, and lacquer into a single vision. It remains proof — whichever bench in Shinshu a given piece was born on — that a workshop inside one of the world's largest watch conglomerates can still produce something as personal, as slow, and as singular as anything to come out of the Vallée de Joux. Credor's current collections — Goldfeather (2023, ultra-thin mechanicals), the Gerald Genta-designed Locomotive (revived 2024 for Credor's 50th anniversary), and the ongoing Masterpiece Collection — carry that inheritance forward, still built across the same two small Shinshu studios that taught themselves haute horlogerie from a shop window and a stranger's kindness in the Vallée de Joux.
