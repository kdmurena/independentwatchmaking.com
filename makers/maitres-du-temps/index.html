Maîtres du Temps

Switzerland

PriceCHF 100,000–300,000+
VolumeVery limited (30–50 pieces per Chapter)

Maîtres du Temps was one of the most original and intellectually compelling collaborative concepts in modern haute horlogerie — a platform that brought together pairs and trios of the finest independent watchmakers to create a single landmark watch, combining their specific and distinct talents into a unified piece that none could have made alone. It was founded in 2005 by Steven Holtzman, an American who had entered horology in 1982 on the sales team of his father's Gruen Marketing Corporation, subsequently moving into the distribution and marketing of high-end Swiss brands including Roger Dubuis SA and Jean Dunand. Having spent years connecting the commercial world to the creative world of Swiss independents, Holtzman conceived a brand built entirely on collaboration: not a manufacture, not a single atelier's work, but a series of 'Chapters' — each Chapter a new watch, each watch the result of a different combination of masters. The philosophy was symbolised by a tree: each branch representing a fresh opportunity, a new collaboration, a future Chapter; as the branches intertwine and the tree bears fruit, so a new timepiece is brought to life. The brand launched publicly in Geneva in 2008 with Chapter One, its first watch, and remained active through approximately 2014, producing three Chapters before closing as a producing entity. The watches are now regarded as some of the most significant collector pieces of their era — genuine horological artefacts that document a precise historical moment when a small group of the world's finest independents were brought together for a single creative purpose.

Chapter One, unveiled at Geneva in 2008, was developed by three watchmakers with complementary and very deliberately chosen skills: Christophe Claret, Roger Dubuis, and Peter Speake-Marin. Holtzman has described the selection: Claret for his unparalleled capacity to create and engineer solutions for complex complications; Dubuis for his traditional perspective from the old school of Geneva watchmaking, providing historical depth; and Speake-Marin for his design sensibility and ability to bridge the technical and aesthetic. The resulting watch was among the most feature-dense in independent watchmaking at that time: tourbillon, monopusher chronograph, retrograde date, retrograde GMT, and two rolling bars — one at 6 o'clock displaying the day of the week, one at 12 o'clock displaying the moon phase — all in a single movement. The indications comprised coaxial central hours and minutes sharing the chronograph 60-second counter; a transparent window revealing the working tourbillon; the day roller at 6 o'clock; retrograde GMT at 9 o'clock; 60-minute chronograph counter and precision moon phase roller at 12 o'clock; and retrograde date at 3 o'clock. Originally 33 pieces were planned, with an additional 17 movements reserved for future service; the production was later expanded to 50 timepieces.

Chapter Two (2009) was developed by Daniel Roth, Peter Speake-Marin, and Roger Dubuis, and took a different approach: a triple calendar wristwatch with a big date at 12 o'clock, month on roller at 12 o'clock, day on roller at 6 o'clock, and subsidiary seconds at 6 o'clock, with automatic winding and adjusting paddles for the day and month rollers. Daniel Roth — who had spent 14 years at Breguet, introducing what is now considered the 'Breguet look' before launching his own brand in 1989 — brought the deep legacy of Geneva watchmaking to bear on the calendar architecture.

Chapter Three Reveal, presented at Baselworld 2012, is universally considered the masterpiece of the series and one of the finest collaborative watches ever made. Its two creators were Kari Voutilainen and Andreas Strehler — a pairing of extraordinary complementary genius. Voutilainen developed the complete movement architecture for what became Calibre SHC03: he designed the bridges and plates, specified the decoration programme, and was personally responsible for the finishing — a Strausmann balance spring with Breguet overcoil, broad-striped plates, exquisite bevelling, polished screws, the full vocabulary of Voutilainen's finishing standard applied to the first original calibre he had ever developed for another brand. Strehler devised the Reveal mechanism itself: the technical interface between movement, complication, and case that gives the watch its name and its central drama. At rest, the Chapter Three appears as a refined, almost modest watch: moon phase at approximately 4:30, date at 2 o'clock, seconds at 8 o'clock, nothing to suggest the complexity concealed within.

Press the pusher at 3 o'clock, and two hidden dial panels first become slightly recessed, then slide open at 12 o'clock and 6 o'clock to reveal large rollers — a day/night indicator at 12 o'clock and second time zone displays at 6 o'clock — dramatically appearing from beneath the dial surface. Through the open caseback, the roller carriages are visible within the movement's architecture, the two-thirds plate movement making space for the rollers at top and bottom of the layout. In a 42mm case with short lugs designed for exceptional wrist comfort, the Chapter Three Reveal combines what are effectively two watches in one: a refined, conventional time display and a completely hidden second-timezone world-time instrument that appears only on demand. Strehler also devised innovative production methods and technical solutions for the custom components required by this mechanism — work that made the production of the 30-piece limited edition viable.

Chapter Three Reveal is traded today privately and through top-tier auction houses; it appears in the collections of watchmakers, collectors, and critics who regard it as one of the defining achievements of the independent watchmaking era.
