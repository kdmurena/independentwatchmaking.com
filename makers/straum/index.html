Straum is Norway's flagship contribution to the new wave of design-led independent watch brands — 'Raw Norwegian Nature. Designed in Norway, Swiss Made.' The Oslo company was founded by two industrial designers, Lasse Roxrud Farstad and Øystein Helle Husby, who met in design school, sharpened their craft at consumer-product giants (their résumés include work for the likes of Philips, Samsung, and Sennheiser), and reconnected in 2017 around a shared conviction: that Norway's raw, elemental landscapes — fjords, glaciers, volcanic rock, and the aurora — deserved the same watchmaking treatment Grand Seiko gives Japan's. Their first collection, the Opphav, launched in 2021 and proved the concept; reviewers praised how completely the founders' ideas had been 'lifted off the page and brought to life in the metal.' But it was the second act that made the brand's name — and it began with an actual expedition. Straum assembled a crew and sailed five days across the Arctic from Svalbard to Jan Mayen, the remote 55km volcanic island 900km northeast of the mainland (population: a couple dozen military and meteorological personnel), trekking over glacial caves toward Haakon VII Toppen, the barely marked summit of Beerenberg — the world's northernmost stratovolcano. The textures and colors they documented became the DNA of the Jan Mayen collection, launched in 2023 and chronicled in a brand documentary.

The Jan Mayen is Straum's definitive statement: an integrated-bracelet sports watch of moderate, wearable proportions (roughly 38mm × 9.7mm, 100m water resistance, screw-down crown, double-domed AR sapphire front and back) whose deeply sculpted dials read like core samples of the island itself — vertical striations resembling wind-packed snow on the black and white versions, radial grooves with fumé gradients on the colored ones, plus limited editions like the Purple Borealis and Aurora Sky gradients (200 pieces each), the Glacial Rift drawn from the team's own photographs of the glacier caves, and a sold-out red-dial collaboration with Fratello. Rather than the expected Sellita, Straum chose the La Joux-Perret G101 — the upmarket Swiss automatic with a 68-hour power reserve at 28,800vph that reviewers have called the movement of choice for serious modern independents. Obsessive engineering runs through the details, showcased in the brand's 'Microns Matter' video series: a pin-and-collar bracelet built to unusually tight tolerances so the links nest seamlessly, an optional micro-adjust butterfly clasp, clever adapters that let the integrated case accept any standard 20mm strap, and an included 'Straum Capsule' travel case machined from a single block of hard-anodized aluminium.

The range has since expanded upward through titanium: grade 5 titanium cases (a restrained $300 premium over steel), the Arctic editions, and — newest of all — the Frozen Metal Titanium ($2,950), which introduced the brand's first full titanium bracelet along with a frosty white dial and polished 'Frostbite Blue' PVD accents whose uniformity, the brand notes, demands painstaking component finishing and precise coating chemistry. Prices run from roughly $1,700 for the steel Jan Mayen to $2,950 for the Frozen Metal on its bracelet, sold directly worldwide with fulfillment from Norway and the U.S. Press reception has been emphatic — aBlogtoWatch, WatchTime, Fratello, and Watchfinder have all covered the brand, with one reviewer summing up the proposition as the integrated-bracelet appeal of a Nautilus, the nature-textured dials of a Grand Seiko, and entry-level-Longines accessibility — from a young Norwegian house whose founders climb the volcanoes they put on your wrist.
