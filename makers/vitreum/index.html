Vitreum was founded in Randers, Denmark, by Kasper Reisner, who set out to build a watch brand around one of horology's most demanding decorative crafts, grand feu enamel, rather than around a movement or a case. Every dial in the collection begins as a solid disc of sterling silver, individually hand-engraved before being built up in fine layers of vitreous enamel and fired, ground, and polished repeatedly to achieve its final depth of colour — a process that can take a single dial anywhere from days to, for the most ambitious pieces, well over a week. All of Vitreum's watches share the same platform, the FH01: a 39.5mm stainless steel case, 46.5mm lug-to-lug and 9.5mm thick, powered by the hand-wound Sellita SW210-1, with a sapphire crystal under multi-layer anti-reflective coating and a solid, deep-etched caseback.

The brand's numerals are its own invention: rather than draw on familiar modern minimalism, Reisner traced Scandinavian numeral history back to the Hauksbók, an Icelandic manuscript from around 1310 containing some of the earliest known uses of Hindu-Arabic numerals in the region, and built an original typeface from that inspiration. Vitreum's original Core Collection — Arctic Blue, Firebird, Emerald Green, Tempest Grey, and Dark Skies, each a single-colour enamel dial over a distinct hand-engraved texture — sold out steadily and has since been retired to make room for newer work, with Underwood, a quieter, more muted green design released in small, deliberately irregular batches, now standing as the brand's more accessible current offering at €3,000.

Above that sits the Rare Handcrafts collection, Vitreum's most ambitious dials, each priced around €5,000. Starry Night, a grand feu homage to Van Gogh, carves the painting's swirling brushwork directly into sterling silver with a graver and sets its moon and stars in shaped 24k gold foil, with a second run limited to just ten pieces. Ammonite renders the crystalline interior of a fossilised ammonite shell in warm golds and mahogany enamel, its hand-carved chambers evoking the same golden-ratio spiral found throughout nature, and was later revisited as the one-off Legacy Ammonite. Honeycomb uses the champlevé technique — engraving and filling hexagonal cells with fine layers of enamel — to build a geometric honeycomb pattern, its second version selling out its full run of 50 pieces in a single release. Storm & Sea, produced in only five pieces, applies transparent enamel over a hand-hammered silver dial evoking mist over the sea off the Welsh coast, where several of Vitreum's dials are made, with a nod to Edo-period Japanese wave art.

Vitreum operates The Playground as a separate, smaller channel for finished experimental and one-off pieces — watches born from testing new colours or techniques that never quite fit an official collection, sold as-is rather than made to order. Employing around five people and producing only a handful of watches a year, the brand has drawn early recognition from the Grand Prix d'Horlogerie de Genève, with the FH01 Emerald Green among the watches submitted for consideration, and has been compared by reviewers to the Scottish enamel house Anordain — though, as one reviewer noted, without Anordain's long waitlists.
