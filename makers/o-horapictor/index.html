O&HoraPictor grew out of decades of founder Eriks's focused exploration of vitreous enamel and his parallel love of horology. That long study led him, in 2017, to establish his own dedicated enamel atelier — a space built purely for studying and practicing an ancient, fire-formed craft on its own terms, well before any watch brand existed around it. O&HoraPictor itself was born only in 2024, growing naturally out of years spent simply observing how enamel behaves under heat, and the atelier's stated mission since has been to translate that hard-won material understanding into a contemporary language of watchmaking — giving an ancient craft a new, wearable form that can travel with its owner for years. The brand describes its approach as standing at the meeting point of traditional craftsmanship and contemporary aesthetics, continually exploring how an ancient material might speak in a different voice without abandoning the reverence the craft demands.

Every O&HoraPictor dial begins with raw enamel ground by hand in an agate mortar and pestle into a fine, uniform powder — a process taking three to four hours on its own — then repeatedly washed to remove impurities and ensure clarity once fired. The powder is applied to the dial base in at least eight separate layers, each one fired in a kiln at roughly 800°C, with uniform thickness essential at every stage since even small variations can produce bubbles or other firing defects. Because different enamel colors react to heat differently, and because factors like temperature shift, enamel thickness, metal expansion, and the behavior of color oxides can all alter or ruin a result in progress, no two finished dials are ever quite identical — mastery here comes from hands-on understanding built over years rather than any fixed formula. Beyond straightforward color work, the atelier extends the language of enamel through silver leaf application, hand engraving beneath transparent enamel, and structural silver wire and ring inlays that divide a dial into distinct color fields — cloisonné technique at its most demanding can mean a single dial built from over 100 individually wired and enameled cells, finished afterward by hand-polishing under running water. Hands, too, are made in-house and heat-blued the traditional way, rested on a bed of copper shavings for even heat distribution as the steel shifts color.

The current collection includes Totem Cipher, a cloisonné enamel dial with numerals inspired by the Rosetta Stone as a nod to ancient civilization; Volcano Grey, a silver leaf enamel dial inspired by submarine volcanic eruptions, blending ocean and molten lava into a single grey field; Yellow Track, an opaque, dry-sifted enamel dial pairing a matte surface and track-style layout for a retro-meets-dynamic look; and Galaxy, a mineral-mixed black enamel dial whose fired surface carries a quiet, shimmering radiance evoking deep space. Each model channels a different facet of the atelier's enameling vocabulary, but all share the same underlying commitment: that the difficulty of the craft is not an obstacle to be minimized, but the very thing that defines serious, uncompromising watchmaking.
