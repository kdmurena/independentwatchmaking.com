Founded in 1993 by Andreas Hentschel — watchmaker, founder, and designer — who trained at the Hamburg watchmakers school (founded 28 April 1878, three days before Glashütte's school, one of the oldest in Germany). Hamburg has a 200-year tradition in fine horology: at its peak it housed more chronometer makers than Geneva, sustained by its great shipping port and the 1875 German Naval Observatory, Naval Institute, and timeball device. Johann Heinrich Kessels — Hamburg's most famous chronometer maker — learned his trade from A.-L. Breguet himself, and his signature small seconds at 6 o'clock is an homage honoured in all Hentschel hand-wound watches. Hentschel continues this Hanseatic chronometric heritage: 'hanseatic understatement, uncompromising precision, timeless elegance.'

Every watch is built step by step by a single watchmaker in the Hamburg atelier — entirely by hand, in small numbers, on individual order (3–6 months). All components are either crafted in-house or produced exclusively for Hentschel; no off-the-shelf parts shared with other brands. Vintage NOS (new-old-stock) movements from the 1930s–1950s or the in-house Cal. Werk 1 are used. Customers choose the movement, year of manufacture, case shape, dial, and finishing. Each watch is personally signed by the watchmaker. A photo documentation booklet of over 100 images recording every stage of that individual watch's creation accompanies every piece. Three case shapes: H1 (extra-flat dress watch, 39.5mm and 37mm), H2 Klassik, and H2 Hafenmeister (harbor master), available in steel, bronze (Hamburg Botschafter), rose gold, white gold, or platinum. Producing 150–200 pieces per year, exclusively on request.

The buying experience is as Hanseatic as the watches. Hentschel sells only direct from the atelier in Hamburg-Eppendorf — a personal fitting and consultation in the manufacture, or a sample-watch service that sends model watches to the customer's home with telephone advice — and the customisation runs deep into the movement itself: clients choose whether their calibre carries a swan-neck fine regulator, whether the jewels sit in gold chatons, and whether the vintage NOS ébauches (typically fine old Unitas and AS calibres, meticulously refinished) are plated in red, white, or yellow gold, before selecting hands, dial, and case. The Hafenmeister that anchors the H2 line wears heat-blued leaf hands over a fine cream dial beneath a high-domed, double-anti-reflective sapphire at an ideal 38.7 mm, while the extra-flat H1 1877 — named for the founding era of Hamburg's watchmaking school — pairs a slim precious-metal case with the manufacture calibre Werk 1, and an H1 Chronometer variant carries the observatory tradition forward explicitly. The clientele says something about the appeal: mountaineer Reinhold Messner treasures the hand-built Hentschel he calls his Nanga Parbat watch, ranking it with his legendary 1978 solo of the Diamir face, and German actors number among the devoted owners who fill collector forums with near-reverent posts — a common refrain being that the watches must be held in the hand to be understood, their symbiosis of blued hands, fine dials, and domed crystals resisting photography. In an industry of boutiques and waitlists, Hentschel remains what it was in 1993: one atelier, one telephone number, and a watchmaker who builds your watch after you ask him to.
