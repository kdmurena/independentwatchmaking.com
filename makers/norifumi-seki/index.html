Norifumi Seki first grew curious about watchmaking around age 17, after buying a watch with money from a part-time job and wondering how it actually worked. A documentary he saw around the same time, on fellow Japanese independent watchmaker Masahiro Kikuno, planted the idea that a single person could genuinely build one; he began researching the craft, bought a copy of George Daniels' writings, and enrolled at the Hiko Mizuno Watchmaking School in Tokyo. In his optional fourth year there, dedicated to an independent graduation project, Seki built a spherical moonphase pocket watch — rather than a conventional flat disc, an actual rotating sphere in two materials displays the moon's phase, paired with a compact rotating-drum calendar display in place of standard discs; roughly half the movement's gear train came from a Valjoux 7750 base, but Seki hand-made the balance wheel and designed the moonphase and calendar mechanisms entirely himself. Photos of his student work, posted to a forum, caught the attention of watch journalist SJX, who interviewed him and personally encouraged him to enter the F.P. Journe Young Talent Competition — a prize Seki, only in his early twenties and doubtful he even qualified, did not expect to win. He did, in 2020, becoming one of the youngest watchmakers ever admitted into the AHCI through the award. Kikuno, whose own documentary had started it all, later became one of his actual teachers; Seki counts fellow Japanese independents Hajime Asaoka and Naoya Hida among his closest mentors today.

That unexpected win, and the attention it brought, led American entrepreneurs HK Ueda and Johnny Ting — former startup colleagues with backgrounds in automotive design and user experience — to reach out during the COVID-19 pandemic. Seki's first instinct was to decline; he took the initial meeting mostly out of politeness. It took roughly a year of patient, long-distance conversation, conducted largely over video calls while international travel was restricted, before he agreed to join them. What ultimately persuaded him was less the specific idea than the broader opportunity it represented: a genuine chance to design a watch entirely from scratch around a real philosophical question, rather than simply execute a styling brief. The three founded Quiet Club together — Ueda as CEO, Ting as Head of Design, Seki as Head of Watchmaking — built around a shared belief that a watch's job is to create a deliberate boundary between focused, purposeful work and the noise of the outside world. The brand takes its name directly from that idea, and operates from a small workshop in Sumida City, Tokyo.

Quiet Club's debut watch, the QC01 Fading Hours, unveiled in 2024, gives that philosophy mechanical form: a genuinely novel mechanical alarm, gentler and more melodic than the sharp buzz of a Vulcain Cricket or Jaeger-LeCoultre Memovox, built around a chiming system Seki engineered from first principles. Rather than a conventional gong, the watch's entire visible dial doubles as the sounding plate, struck from behind by a vertically mounted hammer — a first-of-its-kind construction that left Seki designing and hand-finishing nearly every component himself, with only a handful of basic parts sourced off the shelf. Operation runs through a single button integrated into the crown, which sets, starts, and cancels the alarm in sequence while simultaneously recharging the striking mechanism with every press; a dedicated, normally hidden pair of alarm hands (in the manner of a rattrapante) appears only when the alarm is engaged, set via the rotating bezel. The dial itself, in hand-polished and sand-blasted German silver with Grand Feu enamel hour and minute markers, carries no logo or branding anywhere — its only signature is a Morse-code 'Q' and 'C' worked into the 9 and 3 o'clock markers. Cased in a deliberately bowl-shaped grade 5 titanium case (40.3mm wide, 44mm lug-to-lug, sapphire front and back) that tucks its lugs inward for a smaller effective footprint, the hand-wound movement runs at 2.5Hz with 50 hours of reserve. Limited to an initial allocation of 30 pieces at USD 85,000 each, with roughly ten built in the first year, Fading Hours carried Quiet Club to the semi-finals of the 2025–2026 Louis Vuitton Watch Prize for Independent Watchmaking. Seki has said he intends to keep building further concepts under the Quiet Club name for now, while holding onto a longer-term ambition — one day, when the vision is right — to found a watchmaking house entirely under his own name.
