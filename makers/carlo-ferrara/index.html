Carlo Ferrara (1943–2012) was an Italian watchmaker who learned the trade from his own father, also a watchmaker, before developing an unconventional display of his own in the early 1990s: rather than sharing a central axis, the hour and minute hands each rode along their own independent, oval racetrack-shaped path — minutes on the left half of the dial, hours on the right — with only the seconds hand mounted centrally. Ferrara called the format 'Regulatore,' after the antique regulator-clock tradition of giving each hand its own scale, and one version was even nicknamed the 'Regulatore Jockey' for its resemblance to an actual horse track; the effect, often described since as a 'dance' between the two hands, gave the watches their lasting nickname of 'dancing hours.'

Achieving that display demanded a substantial reworking of the movement: no other watch asks a hand to rotate 180 degrees, drop vertically while staying horizontal, rotate another 180 degrees, and rise again, so Ferrara devised a D-shaped, internally toothed gear to guide each hand through the sequence. The mechanism was built around a heavily modified ETA 2892-A2 base, referred to internally as the calibre CF100, finished with blued screws, Geneva stripes, and an engine-turned mainplate, and — according to the brand's own historical marketing — carried the Geneva Seal.

Cases were offered in stainless steel or 18k gold, with elegantly curled lugs and screw-secured bracelet links, some of them inner-engraved with the brand's crossed-keys logo; casebacks were either solid or sapphire, and because the hand mechanism itself required extra depth, certain references set a small magnifying lens over the recessed date window to keep it legible. The sportier Regulatore Sport ran 39.5mm across and 10.5mm thick, with Super-LumiNova-filled hands, a steel bracelet, and a display back. At its peak the workshop handmade around 1,500 watches a year, sold through dealers across the United States, Japan, Italy, Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Hong Kong, with Ferrara's son Sergio later stepping in to help grow the business. Carlo Ferrara died in 2012, and the brand he founded no longer operates — one of the more overlooked chapters in Italian watchmaking, largely forgotten outside a small circle of regulator-display collectors; the ETA-based movement remains straightforward to service, and surviving examples of the Regulatore family typically trade on the secondary market today for between 1,500 and 3,000 USD.
