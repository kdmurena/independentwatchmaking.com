Giuliano Mazzuoli is Florence's designer-watchmaker — a man who, when people call him 'architect,' replies that he has no degree and asks that no title be placed before his name. For more than seventy years he has lived and worked in the hills around Florence (the company sits in Barberino Tavarnelle, in the Chianti countryside), and horology runs deeper in his blood than even his admirers suspect: his ancestors built tower clocks, documented in a book of 1833, with graffiti drawings of tower clocks still visible on the walls of an old family house in Tavarnelle Val di Pesa and a clock they built in 1757 still in place at Palazzuolo's Villa Salvini. Giuliano himself came to watches sideways — through the family printing business and through design, which he says he discovered late, sharpening his eye among the artisan workshops of Florence and first winning international attention with writing instruments inspired by everyday industrial objects. That instinct — finding 'the tremendous beauty of simplicity' in workshop tools and machines — became his design signature, and in 2004 it produced a cult icon.

That icon is the Manometro ('pressure gauge'): a watch that transplants the rugged aesthetic of the pressure gauges found in workshops of yesteryear onto the wrist — a solid cylindrical 45mm × 14mm stainless steel case with a right-angled base like an instrument housing, an off-set crown, and a dial that reads like an industrial meter, blending mechanical precision with industrial charm and post-modern essentialism. Powered by Swiss movements and priced from €3,600, it became one of the great Italian design watches of the 2000s — famously spotted on the wrist of Brad Pitt — and grew into a family: the sunray-dialed Manometro Soleil, the Manometro Chronograph, the patinating Manometro Bronze, and special editions such as the Manometro Dante honoring Florence's supreme poet. The house motto distills the philosophy: 'time without pressure.'

Two further collections extend the object-inspired language. The Trasmissione Meccanica translates Mazzuoli's love of classic motoring into a chronograph whose forms and controls are drawn from the gearbox and mechanical transmission of vintage Alfa Romeo racers — down to a crown and pushers evoking gear selectors — pure Italian officina culture for the wrist. And the Carrara achieves something almost no one else attempts: a watch case sculpted from genuine white Carrara marble — the very stone of Michelangelo's David — each case unique in its veining, uniting Tuscany's two immortal materials, marble and time. Everything is designed by Giuliano personally, made in Italy, delivered in hand-stitched leather boxes of his own design, and sold through a select network of retailers and the online boutique — the compact, uncompromising output of a Florentine who proves that the deepest watch ideas can come from a pressure gauge, a gear lever, and a block of marble.
