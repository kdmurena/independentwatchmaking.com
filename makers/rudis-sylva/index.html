Rudis Sylva's name is a piece of Jura folklore made into a watch brand. On 17 November 1384, Imier de Ramstein, Prince-Bishop of Basel, granted a tax exemption to anyone willing to clear the dense fir forest crowning the Jura's high plateau — a thousand metres up, home to wolves and unforgiving winters. Jean Ruedin took up the challenge, and the settlement his axes carved out became Les Bois ('The Woods'); Rudis Sylva itself derives from Ruedin's name fused with sylva, Latin for forest. Those harsh winters pushed the new farmers toward trades, and by the 17th century toward watch components specifically — a lineage that runs remarkably deep: the Huot family's escapement business, founded in Les Bois in 1852, is now Nivarox-FAR; the Beaumann family made a third of the world's cylinder escapements by 1937 before pivoting, astonishingly, to inventing the mechanical parking meter (80 million sold worldwide); and the Baume brothers founded their own watch company in Les Bois in 1830, later becoming Baume & Mercier after Paul Mercier joined — meaning two storied Swiss watchmaking names, Rudis Sylva and Baume & Mercier, trace their roots to the very same small village. The modern brand, based in Les Bois to this day, carries that history literally on its case: its logo depicts three fir trees on three mountains, symbolizing the land-clearing of 1384.

Revealed at Baselworld 2009, Rudis Sylva's defining invention is the Harmonious Oscillator — acclaimed by master watchmakers, in the brand's own telling, as the most significant regulating innovation since Breguet's tourbillon in 1801, and the patented brainchild of grand-complications watchmaker Mika Rissanen. Where a tourbillon needs a full minute, rotating through every position, to average out the effects of gravity, the Harmonious Oscillator corrects instantaneously: two complete toothed balances, mechanically interlinked so they share identical amplitude, are driven by a single escapement (one pallet fork positioned at 90 degrees) with their two flat balance springs deployed in constant opposition — a design that deliberately contradicts classical watchmaking theory, which had always sought to isolate a balance wheel from mechanical stress rather than link it to another. The result, patent no. 700747, beats at 21,600vph (3Hz), completes a full 360-degree cycle every 60 seconds across a 17.4mm cage, and delivers roughly 70–72 hours of reserve from manual winding — with, its makers claim, superior amplitude consistency in every position compared to a conventional tourbillon or carrousel.

Every Rudis Sylva case (44mm, in rose gold or titanium, such as the RS23) displays the double balance at 6 o'clock, framed by delta hands and Roman numerals on richly layered dials, with the entire movement — bridges, gear train, barrel, oscillator bridge — finished to full haute horlogerie standard: hand bevelling, circular graining, diamond-polished corners, snailed barrels, and hand guilloché or flinqué work on the oscillator bridge itself. Decoration draws on a constellation of named Jura artisans: hand guilloché by Georges Brodbeck in Saignelégier, engraving by Sylvain Bettex and Bertrand Degiorgi, and enamelling by Sophie Cattin Morales. The catalogue runs from the RS05 through the RS10, RS12, RS14, RS16, and flagship RS23, alongside a dedicated special-order program for clients wanting a bespoke execution — each piece, at heart, a mechanical argument that a village of woodcutters, forest-clearers, and farmers-turned-watchmakers still has something genuinely new to teach Swiss haute horlogerie six centuries later.
