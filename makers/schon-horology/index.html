Founded in 2018 by Ian Schon — a Boston University mechanical engineer (class of 2012), former corporate product designer, and self-described 'slow watchmaker.' Ian began by designing cases for old movements, then started making dials and hands, driven by a philosophy called 'slow watchmaking' — applying the Slow Movement (which transformed food, fashion, and design) to horology: rejecting automation in favour of quality and taking one's time. His mentor: Nicholas P. Trahadias II, a Swiss-trained high-end watch repair specialist in Boston's Jewellers Building. When Trahadias saw Ian's work, he immediately knew he would succeed.

Each watch takes 100+ hours of painstaking work. Cases, dials, and hands are designed, engineered, and manufactured by Ian himself in-house or locally in Boston, in steel or titanium. Ian also runs Schon Design (schondsgn.com), making precision pens and other objects with the same philosophy. Three key models: the Dot (rotating sub-seconds visible through a window at 6 o'clock), the Cardinal (time-only, clean dial), and the Prismatic (unique prismatic case geometry). In an era where 10 watchmakers retire in the US for every one who enters, Ian Schon is a rare exception — a Millennial horologist making watches from scratch in America.

Schon's path into the craft began with frustration rather than ambition: in 2013 he bought a watch online that arrived nothing like advertised, took it apart out of curiosity, and used the metalworking skills from his mechanical engineering degree to machine it a proper new case. Before opening his own studio, he spent time as a senior product designer at IDEO, one of the world's most respected design consultancies — training in user-centred design that shows up in how deliberately each Schon piece is considered before a single cut is made. He has since built two watches for Hodinkee and spent a stretch developing an original jump-hour module, but has been candid that the attention such projects bring isn't always welcome: increased visibility, he's said, brought as much criticism (that his watches were too expensive, that he should be building his own movement from scratch) as it did new demand, and he decided deliberately to stay small rather than chase scale. Trahadias, his mentor, makes the useful distinction between the two men's crafts: a watchmaker repairs and refurbishes, while what Schon does — designing and building watches from nothing — makes him, properly speaking, a horologist, a title Trahadias calls genuinely rare in the United States. Every watch remains stamped simply 'Schon' on the back, a mark Ian has said reflects exactly what the brand is: not a company he's building toward an exit, just one engineer, still making these by hand, one hour at a time.
