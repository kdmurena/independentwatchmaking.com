Official Site ↗

Christian Klings is one of the purest expressions of the lone artisan watchmaker — born in Dresden in 1957, a member of the AHCI since 2009, and long based in small-town Germany (first Wechselburg in Saxony, later Saalfeld in Thuringia), whom SJX called 'the quintessential independent watchmaker as imagined by most watch collectors: an artisan working on his own, building highly complex and beautiful timepieces from scratch.' Note for collectors: Klings has officially retired and is no longer accepting new commissions, which places his tiny lifetime output — roughly twenty pieces across some three decades — firmly in the realm of the secondary market and makes each surviving watch a closed chapter of independent horology. His career was guided by two missions: to push the limits of traditional mechanical escapement technology, and to create refined timepieces through traditional methods rooted in the ingenuity of 17th- and 18th-century watchmaking. Every component of every watch — plates, bridges, wheels, case, crown, gaskets, even the dial — was designed on paper and made by hand with traditional hand-driven tools; like a sculptor, Klings shaped bridges, hands, levers, and springs with often nothing more than a file and a polishing stick, so each piece bears the slight variances of true handwork in its Geneva stripes and guilloché. As the AHCI's own profile wryly notes, some of his complicated timepieces may appeal even more to the scientist than to the watch collector.

His escapement research produced his crowning achievement: the self-starting spring detent escapement, an accomplishment SJX described as 'historically impossible.' The detent (chronometer) escapement — efficient, lubrication-free, the standard of marine chronometers, and traditionally the first major project of independents from Voutilainen to F.P. Journe — suffers one ancient flaw: it stops when jarred and cannot restart itself. Klings solved it with an extra pair of springs connected to the gear train that lock the balance when the mainspring runs empty and release it upon winding. He never patented the invention; the technical diagrams pass only to the owner of the Tourbillon Nr. 2, the 47mm yellow gold pocket watch (case made in-house, sterling silver dial hand-engraved) that embodies it — begun in 2001, perfected through fourteen years of trial and error, and absorbing an estimated 1,500–2,000 hours of work on a heavily modified 19th-century Swiss calibre with a one-minute tourbillon, open-worked German silver bridges, moon phase, and his proprietary zero-reset hacking seconds that snaps the seconds hand to 12 when the crown is pulled. His tourbillon wristwatches carried the research to the wrist: the Tourbillon No. 4 with free balance escapement and a nearly-unheard-of 10-second cage; the No. 6, a one-minute tourbillon visible from every side; and the Flying Tourbillons No. 7 and No. 18 — the latter a hand-wound one-minute flying tourbillon with power reserve and moon phase over a blue guilloché dial. His shock-resistant experimental escapements bore names of typical wit: the Mosquito, with an innovative pivoted detent, and the Desmodromic, inspired by Ducati's positive-control valve system.

From 2017 until retirement, Klings turned deliberately to simple, elegant time-only watches — classical in style and construction, influenced by Breguet and Daners, offered strictly by commission (from around USD 50,000, with tourbillons from USD 120,000). The series reads like a diary of quiet refinement: the No. 00 inspired by Pacific Ocean waves; the No. 02 with blue silk dial, guilloché subdial, and rose gold details; the stainless steel No. 05 'Visibility' with hand-engraved blued details; the No. 06 and No. 07 with distinctive visible power reserve displays; and the collector-commissioned Open Version 1 & 2, whose hand-wound movements were reduced to beautifully shaped tubular steel bridges polished to a mirror finish over a frosted gilt base plate — four of the 19 jewels in screwed chatons, a large balance evoking 1800s pocket watchmaking, an unusual silk-fabric dial center within a solid silver chapter ring, blued steel hands, and a 40mm white gold case with his signature teardrop lugs. Documented from International WristWatch's 'Solo Artist' profiles (2000–2001) to SJX's chronicles of his inventions, Klings retires as connoisseurs' shorthand for handwork without compromise — a watchmaker whose complete works number fewer pieces than many brands release in a single month.
