Stefan Kudoke

Stefan Kudoke's passion for watchmaking began at an early age when two skilled master craftsmen instilled in him an appreciation for mechanical timekeeping. Though not born into a watchmaking family, he graduated with honours, then refined his skills at Glashütte Original's studio for complications and prototypes, followed by professional development in the service department for Breguet, Blancpain, and Omega in New York. He earned his Master Craftsman Certificate at the remarkable age of 22 — and, bolstered by parallel studies in economics and finance, set out on the independent path of creating his own watches.

His first principle is individuality: each KUDOKE represents a creative collaboration between watchmaker and client, integrating preferences, styles, and personalities to form a truly unique piece. The aspiration towards individuality is at the heart of every creative decision at KUDOKE. Working from Glashütte, he produces hand-made watches with in-house calibres, gold movements, hand-made dials, engraving, and skeletonization. Remarkable value at under €10,000 for a level of handwork that commands multiples elsewhere.

Kudoke's HANDwerk line, launched in 2019, marked his evolution from a maker known chiefly for ornate skeletonised pieces into a serious in-house movement developer: the Kudoke 1 introduced his own calibre with subsidiary seconds, the Kudoke 2 added a 24-hour indication and went on to win the GPHG in 2019, and the Kudoke 3 introduced a distinctive three-armed hour hand. That trajectory culminated in 2025 with the KUDOKE 5, which takes the 24-hour display that was a supporting feature on the K2 and makes it the entire point of the watch — a rethinking the brand describes as reconnecting wearers with the astronomical origins of timekeeping, priced at €17,900. Kudoke became a full AHCI member in 2021, and his seven-person workshop in the small Saxon village of Weifa, a short drive from Glashütte, continues to pass every bridge, plate, hand, and balance cock through hand-finishing and engraving before assembly — work visible in pieces like the playful, fully skeletonised 'KudOktopus' and the 20-piece KUDOKE 3 Nocturne of February 2026, whose two-level black dial deliberately makes the time harder to read at a glance, rewarding patience the way its maker rewards collectors willing to wait for one of his watches. Kudoke has also lent his engraving and finishing expertise to outside collaborations, including work on Louis Erard's regulator collection — proof that Glashütte's grand tradition of three-quarter plates and swan-neck regulators still has room for genuinely personal artistic expression.
