Founded in 1992 by Roland G. Murphy in Mount Joy, Lancaster County, Pennsylvania — the historic heartland of American watchmaking, where Hamilton, Adams & Perry, and other legendary names once made the USA a genuine watchmaking nation. Murphy's story began working part-time at a clock company in high school; he trained at Bowman Technical School in Lancaster, then WOSTEP in Neuchâtel in 1986, one of very few Americans ever accepted. He launched his professional career at Hamilton Watch Co. in 1987, rising to Technical Director of product development before founding RGM in 1992 with a simple, enduring mission: 'Making history in watchmaking' in America. RGM remains the only U.S. watchmaker to manufacture its own cases, parts, and movements domestically — modern CNC machining coexists with antique, hand-operated equipment, and hand-blued screws and hand-polished components sit alongside components cut to the fine tolerances only automation affords.

In 2007 RGM introduced the Caliber 801, the first high-grade mechanical movement produced in series in the United States since Lancaster's own Hamilton stopped making in-house calibres in 1969 — roughly 90% American-made and a genuine watchmaking landmark. The Pennsylvania Tourbillon followed as the first serially produced tourbillon movement made in North America, its Wolf's Tooth winding wheel and Pennsylvania Keystone engraved directly onto the bridge. To mark its 20th anniversary in 2012, RGM introduced Caliber 20, its third fully in-house movement. Guilloché (engine-turning) runs through the whole catalogue, produced in-house on antique rose engines and straight-line engines — a nearly lost art RGM keeps alive, and one it believes lets it make a claim few if any other watch companies can: timepieces designed, assembled, and hand-decorated with guilloché by the very same craftsperson.

Bespoke commission work sits at the center of the business — from simple customizations of a production reference like the Ref. 151 (a unique dial, a personalized engraved rotor) to fully bespoke pieces built entirely in-house around a client's vision: custom cases built for a rare vintage Peseux 260, an IWC movement, or a Patek Philippe movement; pendant tourbillons; dive watches; sports-team tributes for clients from the Washington Nationals to the Baltimore Ravens; and complicated pieces with minute repeaters or tourbillons decorated to order. A virtual museum on RGM's own site preserves the models retired along the way — the William Penn line, an early jump-hour regulator, the naval-tribute USS Constellation, and the Lancaster Chronograph among them — a record the company treats as a working resource for its many repeat customers as much as a look back. Producing roughly 100 watches a year from a team small enough to know every client by name, RGM stands, by wide consensus, as the most important living American manufacture — the sole torchbearer of a watchmaking tradition the country once nearly lost entirely.
