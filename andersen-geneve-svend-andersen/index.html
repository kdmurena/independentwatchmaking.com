Born in Denmark in 1942, Svend Andersen earned his watchmaking diploma at the Royal Technological Institute of Copenhagen before moving to Switzerland in 1963. After working at Gübelin in Geneva, in 1969 he created the legendary Bottle Clock — a fully functioning clock built through the neck of a glass bottle — earning the nickname 'Watchmaker of the Impossible' in the international press. The feat caught Patek Philippe's attention; they crossed the street and asked him to join their Atelier des Grandes Complications, where he spent nine years mastering the most demanding complications. In 1980 he founded ANDERSEN Genève, initially producing replacement gold cases for complicated pocket watches that had lost their originals during wars; collector demand for wristwatches soon followed.

In 1985, Andersen co-founded the AHCI with Vincent Calabrese — the body that champions independent watchmakers worldwide — and received the GPHG Special Prize for this contribution in 2023. The atelier has trained Franck Muller, Felix Baumgartner, and Philippe Quentin. World time is the house's signature complication, with one Andersen Worldtimer holding the Guinness Record as the thinnest world time watch ever made; other key complications include the Annual Calendar, Perpetual Calendar, Secular Perpetual Calendar, and — most recently — the Rattrapante Mondiale (2025), the first wristwatch ever to combine a split-seconds chronograph with a worldtimer, in 38.8mm platinum limited to 8 pieces, alongside the Communication 45 (a 2025 GPHG finalist marking the house's 45th anniversary). Equally singular is the Montre à Tact, Andersen's reinterpretation of a complication Abraham-Louis Breguet devised around 1799 for aristocratic clients who considered it impolite to consult a pocket watch in company — Breguet's exterior touch-hand became, on Andersen's wrist version, a numerical wandering-hours display discreetly set between the lugs, readable at a glance without moving hand or wrist. The case itself is a hand-made 'Empire'-form masterpiece — 42mm, 9.8mm thick, its fluted band inspired by Breguet's own pocket watches, entirely free of a crown since the setting mechanism is instead recessed into the caseback under a patented system, meaning the watch literally cannot be adjusted until it is removed, in private. With the dial freed from hands or numerals entirely, each Montre à Tact becomes a bespoke artistic canvas: miniature painting, guilloché, marquetry, engraving, enamelling, and BlueGold — Andersen's own proprietary heat-tempering technique for 24k gold, first developed for this very collection — have all been deployed by a network of Switzerland's finest artisans, including longtime collaborator Kees Engelbarts, whose freehand engraving for a 2019 Only Watch piece recreated a dusk view of Geneva using fossilised mammoth ivory and 0.40mm mother-of-pearl discs.

Collaboration runs through the house's entire identity. Over 45 years, ANDERSEN Genève has partnered with Cartier (on editions of its own Jour et Nuit complication), Chopard (the Lunar d'Oro retrograde perpetual calendar, 2005), revered Japanese retailer Shellman, British heritage house Asprey (the 24-piece Heures du Monde, featuring Asprey's own historic diamond-form guilloché), Danish retailer Ole Mathieson (special Worldtimer and Jumping Hours editions), and fellow independent Konstantin Chaykin, whose Automaton Joker paired his own dual-sided creation with Andersen's expertise in miniature automata. Across more than four decades, only around 1,300 Andersen watches have ever been made — roughly 50 a year — each one a collaboration between Andersen's own team, the client, and a network of artisans, in the tradition of a watchmaker who has spent his entire career treating the impossible as a starting point rather than an obstacle.
